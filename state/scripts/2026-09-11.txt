Shopify's engineering team announced this week that it has fully rebuilt its Shop app, moving away from React Native and into native Swift for iOS and native Kotlin for Android. According to a Shopify Engineering post published Thursday, the entire process, from proof of concept to a published app in the app stores, took twelve weeks, and it was done with a team of just six engineers working alongside AI coding agents.

This matters because Shopify made a very public bet on React Native back in twenty twenty, betting that a single shared codebase across iOS and Android would save time and money compared with writing native code twice. According to the company's own account, six years later that bet has been reversed, and the reason given is specifically the emergence of capable AI coding agents. The logic, as Shopify describes it, is that building native apps twice used to be expensive because it meant duplicating engineering work across two platforms. Now, with AI agents doing much of that duplicate work automatically, native development has become cheaper than maintaining a single shared codebase that still requires bridging and compromise.

The technical piece that made this possible, according to Shopify, is a new internal architecture called Helix, which decouples the app's business logic and exposes it through a command line interface. Normally, testing a small code change on a mobile app means compiling the app and launching a slow simulator, a process that can take minutes and breaks the kind of rapid iteration AI agents rely on. With Helix, agents can test changes against the business logic directly through the CLI in a matter of milliseconds, without ever opening a simulator. That speed, Shopify says, is what allowed continuous, checkpoint-validated rewriting of the app, essentially letting agents propose changes, verify them instantly, and move on, over and over, at a pace that would be impossible with human engineers alone running full builds each time.

The results Shopify is reporting are substantial. The Android version of the app now starts up fifty percent faster from a cold launch. The crash rate has dropped by ninety percent. And the overall app size has shrunk by one hundred nine megabytes. Shopify says it is now applying the same approach to its main Shopify app, which spans more than three hundred screens, a far larger undertaking than the Shop app, and that the rest of its app fleet is expected to follow the same path.

In AI models, DeepSeek released a new model called V4.1-Flash on September tenth, according to the company's API changelog and reporting from The Next Web. The model is a five hundred fifty-two billion parameter mixture of experts system with native image understanding, using eight billion active parameters on input and sixteen billion on output. Starting September fourteenth, DeepSeek says all requests sent to its existing flagship model, V4-Pro, will automatically be rerouted to V4.1-Flash instead, and billed at the cheaper Flash pricing, which runs eleven to fifty-seven percent less depending on the type of tokens being processed. This rerouting will stay in place until DeepSeek ships a successor called V4.1-Pro.

DeepSeek has also published the model's weights openly on Hugging Face under an MIT license. According to the company's internal testing, and corroborated by outside evaluators cited in The Next Web's reporting, V4.1-Flash actually outperforms the older V4-Pro on performance, cost, speed, and latency, which is why DeepSeek is comfortable routing paying customers away from its former flagship model and into what was originally positioned as a lighter, cheaper option. This is a notable move because it means DeepSeek is effectively retiring a flagship product not by shutting it down, but by quietly replacing it under the hood with something smaller and cheaper that happens to perform better. It also reinforces a broader pattern in AI development right now, where mixture of experts designs, which activate only a fraction of their total parameters for any given request, are proving that efficiency and capability are no longer in tension the way they used to be.

Now, a deeper look at today's top story. Shopify's rewrite of its Shop app is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is not just a technical migration story, it is a signal about how software companies may approach engineering decisions going forward. The React Native versus native debate has been running in the mobile development world for close to a decade. React Native, and frameworks like it, let companies write one codebase in JavaScript and deploy it to both iOS and Android, saving on engineering headcount at the cost of some performance and platform-specific polish. Fully native development, using Swift for Apple's ecosystem and Kotlin for Google's, has always delivered better performance and deeper access to each platform's capabilities, but at roughly double the engineering cost, since essentially every feature has to be built twice by separate specialized teams.

Shopify picked the shared-codebase side of that trade-off in twenty twenty, and by its own account, that was a reasonable choice at the time. What changed, according to the company, is not the frameworks themselves, but the cost structure around building native software twice. AI coding agents, when given the right scaffolding, can apparently now handle a large share of that duplicated work, translating a feature built for iOS into its Android equivalent, or vice versa, fast enough that the labor savings from a shared codebase no longer outweigh the performance and reliability gains of going native.

The architectural detail Shopify highlighted, the Helix system that exposes business logic through a command line interface, is arguably the most transferable lesson here for other engineering organizations. The bottleneck in using AI agents for mobile development has generally not been the intelligence of the models themselves, but the slow feedback loops of mobile testing. Compiling an app and booting a simulator to check whether a single line of code works as intended can take minutes, and that friction multiplies enormously when an AI agent is attempting hundreds or thousands of small changes as part of an iterative rewrite. By building a layer that lets agents validate logic changes in milliseconds without touching a simulator, Shopify essentially removed the primary obstacle to letting agents work at a rapid, continuous pace, while still checking their work at every step through what the company calls checkpoint validation.

The measured outcomes back up the approach, at least so far. A fifty percent improvement in cold startup time on Android and a ninety percent reduction in crashes are not marginal gains, they are the kind of improvements that show up directly in app store ratings and user retention. Trimming one hundred nine megabytes off the app's footprint also matters for users on limited storage or slower connections, particularly in markets outside the United States where Shopify's merchant and shopper base is growing.

What happens next will be the real test of whether this was a one-off success or a repeatable method. Shopify says it is now applying the same rewrite process to its main Shopify app, which is a far bigger and more complex piece of software with over three hundred screens, compared to the relatively contained Shop app. If the twelve-week timeline and the same reliability gains hold up at that scale, it would suggest that AI-agent-driven rewrites are not just useful for smaller, well-defined apps, but for legacy enterprise-grade software as well, which would have implications well beyond Shopify, for any company sitting on an aging codebase and weighing the cost of a full rewrite against incremental patching. Shopify says the rest of its app fleet will follow, which means over the coming months there should be more data points to judge whether this twelve-week result was a fluke of a smaller project or the new normal for how the company builds software.

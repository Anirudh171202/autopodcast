Our top story today concerns a problem that doesn't have a body count or a dollar figure attached to it yet, but according to people who work inside the engineering teams that keep the internet running, it might be one of the more consequential shifts happening in software right now. Sylvain Kalache, who works on incident management at the company Rootly, laid out what he calls a paradox in a blog post published this week. As artificial intelligence tools increasingly handle the routine work of responding to system outages and technical incidents, the human engineers who used to do that work by hand are getting less practice doing it. That sounds fine on the surface, since the whole point of automation is to take tedious work off people's plates. But Kalache's argument, echoed in reporting from InfoQ and from the incident management platform incident.io, is that when a truly novel or severe failure happens, the kind that automated systems can't solve on their own, it's those same engineers who get called in. And increasingly, they're rusty.

Kalache draws a direct comparison to commercial aviation. Airline pilots spend remarkably little time these days actually flying a plane by hand, because autopilot systems handle the vast majority of a flight. But pilots are still required to log regular hours in flight simulators, specifically practicing the rare, high-stakes emergencies that automation can't handle, like an engine failure during takeoff. That structured, mandatory practice is what keeps a pilot sharp for the one time in ten thousand flights when things go seriously wrong. Software engineering, by contrast, has no equivalent requirement. There's no simulator hour mandate for a site reliability engineer.

This isn't actually a new idea. As Kalache points out, a researcher named Lisanne Bainbridge described almost exactly this dynamic back in 1983, in a paper called "Ironies of Automation." Bainbridge's argument, which predates modern computing as we know it, was that automation tends to strip operators of practice on the routine tasks that used to build and maintain their skill, while still leaving those same operators responsible for stepping in when something abnormal happens. In other words, automation doesn't remove human responsibility for failure, it just removes the practice that used to prepare humans for that responsibility. According to the reporting this week, that exact dynamic is now playing out inside software teams that have adopted AI tools for what's often called SRE work, short for site reliability engineering.

The proposed fixes, according to incident.io's research, borrow directly from aviation's playbook. That means deliberately scheduled game days, where teams simulate a major outage in a controlled setting. It means chaos engineering, a practice where teams intentionally break parts of their own systems to see how they respond and to keep engineers' instincts sharp. It means tabletop exercises, where a team talks through a hypothetical incident step by step without an actual system going down. And it means something incident.io compares directly to the FAA's proficiency checks for pilots, structured recurrent training that engineers complete on a schedule, not just when they happen to be assigned to an incident. The core message from all of this reporting is that as AI takes over more of the day-to-day operational toil in software, organizations can't treat that as a reason to stop investing in human skill. If anything, it's a reason to invest more deliberately, because the skills that used to build up automatically, just from doing the job every day, no longer will.

Turning now to a very different kind of story out of the world of cryptocurrency infrastructure. According to CoinDesk and The Hacker News, hackers exploited a software vulnerability on September sixth to drain roughly four thousand Bitcoin, worth about three hundred twenty million dollars at the time, from the Liquid Network, a Bitcoin sidechain built by the company Blockstream. That amounted to about ninety-five percent of the network's reserves. In a twist that's becoming almost a genre of its own in crypto security incidents, the attackers later returned thirty-four hundred of the stolen Bitcoin, worth about two hundred sixty-five million dollars, after Blockstream patched the bug they'd exploited. They kept the remainder, roughly five hundred ninety-eight Bitcoin worth about forty-seven million dollars, and there's no disclosed bounty agreement explaining why that specific amount was kept rather than returned in full.

According to the reporting, this wasn't a case of stolen private keys, which is the more familiar story in crypto hacks. Instead, the vulnerability was in software called Elements, which underlies how the Liquid Network operates. The bug allowed the attackers to create tokens called L-BTC that weren't actually backed by real Bitcoin, and then redeem those fake tokens for genuine Bitcoin through the network's normal withdrawal process. As of today, the Liquid Network remains paused, with its bridge nodes offline while Blockstream and the network's federation members work on further security fixes and figure out how to restore proper backing for the L-BTC currency before bringing the system back online.

In sports: Formula One is heading somewhere it's never been before. According to Wikipedia's race entry and reporting from Crash.net and RacingNews365, the twenty twenty-six Spanish Grand Prix moves this weekend to a brand-new circuit called Madring, located in Madrid. It's the first time Spain has hosted a Formula One race weekend since the sport returned to the country back in nineteen fifty-one, and it's the first appearance for this specific circuit on the F1 calendar. Practice sessions run Friday, September eleventh, at one-thirty and five p.m. local time, with qualifying set for Saturday, September twelfth, at four p.m. Central European time, and the race itself on Sunday, September thirteenth, at three p.m., covering fifty-seven laps.

Heading into the weekend, Kimi Antonelli of Mercedes leads the championship after a dominant stretch of the season that includes wins in Australia and Japan and a strong showing at Monza, the Italian Grand Prix, where he finished ahead of teammate George Russell. With seven races left, both the drivers' and constructors' championships are tightening. What makes this weekend particularly unpredictable, according to RacingNews365, is that nobody has real data on the new venue. The traditional powerhouse teams, Mercedes, Ferrari, and McLaren, will be working with essentially the same blank slate as everyone else, since Madring's fast, banked corners don't resemble any of Spain's previous Formula One venues. That puts an unusual amount of weight on how well each team's engineers can adapt on the fly, rather than lean on prior setup data.

That story comes out of the systems software world, where independent researcher Bartosz Fenski published a sweeping set of benchmarks comparing three major filesystems, bcachefs, btrfs, and ZFS, according to a post on Hacker News dated September 22, 2026. What makes this notable isn't just that another filesystem comparison exists. It's what these benchmarks actually measured. Fenski built his tests around real-world conditions that classic benchmarking tools tend to skip entirely, according to the same post, including how these filesystems behave under snapshot aging, compression, redundancy layouts, encryption overhead, and fsync tail latency when a system is running in a degraded state, meaning a drive has failed or storage is otherwise not operating at full health. This matters because most published filesystem benchmarks test a clean, single drive under ideal conditions, which is not how these filesystems get used in production. Anyone running a home server, a small business file share, or a data-heavy application is far more likely to care about how the system performs after months of use, with snapshots piling up and one drive in a multi-disk array starting to act up, than how it performs on day one with an empty disk.

Now let's turn to that deep dive.

The filesystem benchmarking project from Bartosz Fenski represents something the storage and systems engineering community has been asking for, according to discussion on Hacker News, for a long time: real, reproducible, hardware-based performance data for copy-on-write filesystems under the conditions people actually encounter, rather than the conditions that are easiest to test.

To understand why this matters, it helps to understand what copy-on-write, or CoW, filesystems are and why they've become dominant in modern storage. Btrfs, ZFS, and the newer bcachefs all use a copy-on-write approach, meaning that when data is modified, the filesystem writes the new version to a fresh location on disk rather than overwriting the old data in place. This is what makes features like instant snapshots, checksumming for data integrity, and flexible redundancy layouts possible. But it comes with tradeoffs, and those tradeoffs are exactly what standard benchmarks tend to miss, according to Fenski's published methodology.

Standard benchmarking tools, most notably the ones built into the widely used Phoronix Test Suite, tend to run synthetic, single-device tests measured over a short time window. According to the Hacker News post, this approach systematically misses the performance patterns that actually show up in production deployments. Two of the biggest gaps are snapshot aging and multi-device redundancy. Snapshot aging refers to what happens to filesystem performance as more and more snapshots accumulate over the life of a system, since every snapshot on a CoW filesystem is a reference point the filesystem has to track and account for when writing new data. A filesystem that looks fast on a clean, snapshot-free test can slow down substantially once it's carrying the kind of snapshot history a real user or business would build up over months or years. Multi-device redundancy is the other major factor, since most of these filesystems are frequently deployed across multiple drives in configurations designed to protect against drive failure. How a filesystem performs when it's mirroring or striping data across several disks, and critically, how it performs when one of those disks is degraded or has failed, is a completely different question from how it performs on a single healthy drive, and it's a question that matters enormously to anyone actually relying on this technology to protect their data.

According to the Hacker News post, Fenski also tested fsync tail latency specifically under degraded operation, meaning he measured not the average time it takes for a write to be confirmed as safely stored, but the worst-case outliers, and did so specifically while the storage array was missing a drive or otherwise not operating at full health. Tail latency matters more than average latency for a lot of real workloads, particularly databases, because a single slow outlier can cause a cascading delay that a good average performance number completely hides. Testing this under degraded conditions, rather than only under healthy conditions, gets much closer to worst-case real-world behavior.

The methodology behind these benchmarks is also worth spending time on, because it addresses a common criticism of amateur or informal benchmarking efforts, which is that the underlying hardware or virtualization environment introduces noise that makes results unreliable. According to the post, Fenski obtained physical, dedicated hardware from Hetzner, the German hosting provider, specifically to get real hardware results rather than results from a shared or virtualized environment, which can introduce inconsistent performance due to other tenants competing for the same physical resources. In parallel, he ran more than five hundred ninety separate benchmark runs using GitHub Actions runners, and according to the post, he built in a calibration step specifically to filter out results from virtual machines that showed unreliable or inconsistent performance characteristics. This two-track approach, dedicated physical hardware alongside a large volume of cloud-based runs with a filtering step for noisy results, is a meaningfully more rigorous methodology than what typically shows up in independent filesystem comparisons, which tend to rely on a single machine and a handful of runs.

The results themselves, according to the Hacker News discussion, reveal substantial performance gaps between the three filesystems specifically on multi-device deployments, and specifically for copy-on-write operations and the snapshot aging curves already mentioned. In other words, the filesystems don't just perform differently at the margins. The gaps are large enough to matter for real deployment decisions, and they show up specifically in the scenarios that standard testing tools are not designed to capture.

Why does any of this matter beyond a niche community of systems administrators and storage engineers? Because the choice of filesystem underlies an enormous amount of infrastructure most people never think about, from the servers running cloud storage services to the network-attached storage boxes increasingly common in homes and small offices, to the backend storage for databases that power everyday applications. Btrfs is the default filesystem for several major Linux distributions and underpins a lot of container and virtualization infrastructure. ZFS has been a mainstay in enterprise and archival storage for close to two decades because of its data integrity guarantees. Bcachefs is the newest of the three, recently merged into the mainline Linux kernel, and has been positioning itself as a modern alternative that aims to combine the reliability guarantees of ZFS with performance closer to more traditional filesystems. Decisions about which of these to use get made by engineers designing storage systems that, in turn, affect the reliability and speed of services used by millions of people who will never know or care which filesystem sits underneath their cloud photo backup or their company's internal file server. Benchmark data that actually reflects real degraded-mode, aged, multi-device conditions rather than clean synthetic single-disk tests gives those engineers a meaningfully better basis for that decision than what's existed up to now.

It's also worth noting what this story says about how good technical information sometimes surfaces. This wasn't a corporate benchmark released as part of a product launch, and it wasn't covered by mainstream technology press. It came from an independent researcher publishing his own methodology and raw results, and the initial spread of the work happened through Hacker News, a community built around exactly this kind of technical scrutiny. That's a reminder that a lot of the most useful technical work in software doesn't originate from press releases at all.
